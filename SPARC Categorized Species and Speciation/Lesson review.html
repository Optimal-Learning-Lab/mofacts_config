S01 · Species as distinguishable but changing groups

Pages 434-435 · model

S01. Species as distinguishable but changing groups. Learning demand: Explain how species can be recognizable biological groups even though their members vary and species change through evolution. Required facets: (1) Distinguish variation among members of a group from differences between groups, using simple morphological or genetic cluster plots. (2) Explain that recognizable clustering is evidence of biological grouping, while no single plotted trait is a universal species test. (3) Explain why evolving populations and the splitting of lineages make a universally sharp species definition difficult. S02 · Biological species membership

Pages 435-436 · fact-rule

S02. Biological species membership. Learning demand: State the biological species criterion and apply it to reproductive evidence. Required facets: (1) Recall that the biological species concept concerns actually or potentially interbreeding populations reproductively isolated from other such populations. (2) Require viable, fertile offspring rather than mating or the production of any offspring alone. (3) Apply the criterion to unfamiliar cases, distinguishing reproductive incompatibility from lack of opportunity to meet; geographic separation alone does not establish distinct species. (4) Recognize when reproductive evidence supplied in a case is insufficient to reach a classification. S03. Genetic exchange and species cohesion. Learning demand: Explain how genetic exchange connects populations and can oppose their divergence. Required facets: (1) Explain how reproduction and movement among connected populations allow alleles to spread through a shared gene pool. S04 · When reproductive evidence is usable

Pages 436-438 · rule

S04. When reproductive evidence is usable. Learning demand: Decide whether the biological species criterion is applicable and whether available reproductive evidence is sufficient. Required facets: (1) Recognize that the interbreeding criterion is inapplicable to strictly asexual reproduction and cannot be tested directly in extinct organisms known only from fossils. (2) Distinguish practical barriers to testing from biological evidence: a failed laboratory mating can reflect unnatural conditions, and observing offspring does not yet establish their fertility. (3) Choose a usable kind of evidence for a stated case, drawing on morphology, DNA, ecology, or ancestry when reproductive evidence is unavailable. (4) Distinguish an inapplicable criterion, an inconclusive test, and positive evidence of reproductive isolation. S23 · New species through chromosome-set changes

S23. New species through chromosome-set changes. Learning demand: Distinguish the origins of auto- and allopolyploids and explain how chromosome-set changes can contribute to speciation. S24 · The relationship between selection and speciation

Pages 434, 449-453 · model-rule

S24. The relationship between selection and speciation. Learning demand: Judge whether natural selection is necessary or sufficient for speciation and explain that judgment. Required facets: (1) Distinguish adaptation within a lineage from the origin of reproductively distinct lineages. (2) Explain how divergence involving drift can contribute to reproductive isolation without selection driving that divergence. (3) Explain how different selection pressures can promote divergence and how selection can also occur without a species splitting. (4) Use these mechanisms to reject both the claim that all speciation requires natural selection and the claim that selection always produces new species.
