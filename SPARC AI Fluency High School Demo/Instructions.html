Welcome to AI Fluency

How can you work with AI while keeping your own judgment? In this high-school-level demo, you will explore how AI works, how to communicate what you need, how to evaluate its work, and how to use it responsibly.

How to take part

The tutor will guide you through questions and explanations. Answer in your own words and explain your thinking; you do not need to memorize definitions. Use everyday or school-related examples when they help. If you are unsure, describe what you do understand so the tutor can build on it. The tutor selects what to address next, so the conversation may not follow the list below in order.

Use fictional examples rather than personal or confidential information. Apply your own judgment to AI-generated explanations and check important claims.

What you will learn

Each line below names one knowledge component (KC): a particular idea or skill this lesson develops.

  1. Use Delegation, Description, Discernment, and Diligence together for effective, responsible AI collaboration.
  2. Choose automation, augmentation, or agency to fit the task and human involvement needed.
  3. Explain how generative AI creates content and how generation differs from classification and retrieval.
  4. Explain how data, algorithms, architecture, and computing power support AI capabilities without guaranteeing accuracy.
  5. Distinguish pre-training, later training, and generating a response during use.
  6. Use current instructions and examples to guide AI without assuming the model is being retrained.
  7. Distinguish training knowledge from information available through documents and tools.
  8. Check important claims because fluent, confident AI answers can still be wrong.
  9. Account for limited context when working with long documents and conversations.
  10. Explain why repeated requests can produce different outputs and when that variation is useful.
  11. Evaluate mathematical and logical reasoning without assuming advanced models always succeed.
  12. Clarify the goal, success criteria, and work needed before involving AI.
  13. Compare AI systems on relevant tasks and revisit choices as capabilities change.
  14. Divide work between people and AI according to capabilities and the judgment required.
  15. Describe the desired product, audience, purpose, and relevant constraints.
  16. Provide the background and task information needed for a useful response.
  17. Specify a required workflow, source, or method when the task calls for it.
  18. Describe the conversational role, tone, and level of detail that will help.
  19. Use relevant, correct examples when instructions alone do not communicate the intended pattern.
  20. Recognize when guidance before answering may help and how it differs from an explanation afterward.
  21. Ask AI to help clarify a prompt while checking that it preserves your goal.
  22. Judge the final product for accuracy, usefulness, coherence, and fit to requirements.
  23. Check observable work for reasoning errors, lost constraints, and drift from the agreed method.
  24. Judge whether the assistant communicates and responds to feedback in a useful way.
  25. Give specific corrective feedback that explains the problem and a useful improvement.
  26. Use evaluation to revise instructions, context, tools, or the division of work.
  27. Check privacy, security, permissions, policies, and wider impacts before sharing data with AI.
  28. Disclose AI involvement accurately to the relevant people in a way that fits the context.
  29. Check accuracy, sources, bias, appropriateness, and usage rights before using AI-assisted work.
  30. Write a truthful diligence statement describing AI contributions, review, and human responsibility.
  31. Plan continued practice with specific priorities, activities, and signs of progress.
  32. Maintain reusable prompts and examples, updating them for each new task.

Original source and adaptation

This MoFaCTS SPARC lesson adapts concepts from AI Fluency: Framework & Foundations, developed by Rick Dakan (Ringling College of Art and Design), Joseph Feller (University College Cork), and Anthropic. Its four-part framework is Delegation, Description, Discernment, and Diligence. See also the AI Fluency Framework.

The conversational tutoring format, knowledge-component summaries, and high-school-level presentation are adaptations for this lesson. This demo is not the original course and does not award its certificate.
