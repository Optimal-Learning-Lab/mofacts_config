Welcome to AI Fluency

How can you work with AI while keeping your own judgment? In this high-school-level demo, you will explore how AI works, how to communicate what you need, how to evaluate its work, and how to use it responsibly.

How to take part

The tutor will guide you through questions and explanations. Answer in your own words and explain your thinking; you do not need to memorize definitions. Use everyday or school-related examples when they help. If you are unsure, describe what you do understand so the tutor can build on it. The tutor follows an optimized KC path beginning with matching AI systems to tasks, skips knowledge you have demonstrated, and addresses misconceptions when needed. The list below is a content overview, not the teaching order.

Use fictional examples rather than personal or confidential information. Apply your own judgment to AI-generated explanations and check important claims.

What you will learn

Each line below names one knowledge component (KC): a particular idea or skill this lesson develops.

  1. Collaboration modes: Distinguish automation (AI executes a specified task), augmentation (human and AI develop work together), and agency (AI acts independently on someone’s behalf). Choose according to how settled the outcome is and how much ongoing human participation is needed.
  2. Generation and retrieval: An LLM generates language from learned patterns; it does not necessarily retrieve a stored, verified answer. Distinguish generating content from classifying existing material or retrieving information through tools.
  3. What enabled generative AI: Modern generative AI depends jointly on training data, architectures and algorithms, and computing power. Data supplies examples, architectures organize learning, and computation enables training. Scaling these resources can improve capabilities without guaranteeing correctness on an individual task.
  4. Information access: Distinguish learned knowledge from information supplied through documents and tools. Check whether the assistant has the information and access the task requires. A training cutoff does not prevent using supplied current information, and better wording does not grant missing access.
  5. Confident errors: Generating plausible language does not establish truth. Fluent, confident responses can contain factual errors; confidence alone is insufficient evidence that a claim is correct.
  6. Finite context: An assistant can process only a finite amount of information at once. Earlier or oversized material may no longer guide its response. Account for this limit in long documents and conversations without assuming that all systems handle context and memory identically.
  7. Output variability: Probabilistic generation can produce different answers to repeated requests. Variation can help exploration but complicate consistency; different wording does not establish better accuracy. Some interfaces expose temperature controls, but their availability should not be assumed.
  8. Goals and success criteria: Specify the goal, intended outcome, and criteria for success before involving AI. Include the audience, purpose, scope, format, and constraints that determine whether the result will be usable.
  9. Matching systems to tasks: Compare AI systems on tasks relevant to your intended work. Choose using observed capabilities, limitations, and access requirements; reconsider as systems or needs change. Success on one task does not establish that a system is best for every task.
  10. Necessary context: Supply task information the assistant lacks, including the user’s knowledge level when it affects the answer. Include background that changes what a suitable response should contain; unrelated detail does not help merely by lengthening the prompt.
  11. Required methods: Specify the workflow, sources, sequence, or analytical method when successful execution depends on a particular approach. Requiring a method directs how the work is done; asking for an explanation alone does not establish that the method was followed.
  12. Conversational role: Specify the assistant’s role for the current task: explore alternatives, converge on a decision, challenge an argument, or offer support. State the desired level of detail and tone when they affect useful participation.
  13. Examples: When instructions alone do not convey the desired pattern, provide correct examples of the intended transformation or output, covering relevant variation. Examples demonstrate what to emulate; they are not required for every request.
  14. Help formulating requests: When unsure how to phrase a request, explain the goal and difficulty and ask the assistant to help formulate it. Check that its proposed wording preserves the intended need.
  15. Output evaluation: Evaluate the output against the goal and success criteria: accuracy, coherence, audience fit, requirements, and practical usefulness. Use relevant evidence to decide what to retain, revise, or discard. Checking before release is one application of this evaluation.
  16. Process failures: Inspect observable work for invalid reasoning, lost constraints, unsuitable steps, circularity, fixation, or reintroduced rejected material. Identify the specific step or pattern that conflicts with the agreed approach, especially when the final result is difficult to judge.
  17. Corrective feedback: Identify the response’s specific defect, explain its consequence for the task, and request a concrete correction. Supply revised instructions or examples instead of merely asking the assistant to do better.
  18. Disclosure: Disclose the actual AI contribution to the relevant people, using the timing and detail expected in that setting. Describe what AI did accurately rather than applying one stock disclosure to every situation.
  19. AI-assisted planning: Ask AI to turn a goal, constraints, resources, and timeframe into a plan with concrete steps, milestones, and dependencies. Check the proposed sequence for feasibility and missing work before using it.
  20. Reusable prompts: For recurring tasks, keep reusable instructions and examples with explicit fields for changing information. Fill those fields for each task, evaluate the new result, and update the template when requirements change.

Original source and adaptation

This MoFaCTS SPARC lesson adapts concepts from AI Fluency: Framework & Foundations, developed by Rick Dakan (Ringling College of Art and Design), Joseph Feller (University College Cork), and Anthropic. Its four-part framework is Delegation, Description, Discernment, and Diligence. See also the AI Fluency Framework.

The conversational tutoring format, knowledge-component summaries, and high-school-level presentation are adaptations for this lesson. The AI-assisted planning target was revised by the lesson author. This demo is not the original course and does not award its certificate.
